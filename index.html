01 The cognitive case

Six laws. One conclusion.

The argument for fewer focal points isn't a matter of taste. It's the convergence of six independent findings from cognitive psychology and usability research — all pointing at the same ceiling. Below is each law as documented in the Peak Money UX knowledge base, with the operative application underneath.

LAW 01 · Miller

Working memory has a ceiling

The average person can only keep 7 (±2) items in working memory simultaneously.
Five to nine is the absolute upper bound before saturation — not the goal. Nielsen's recognition heuristic tightens this further to 4–7 elements held at once. Fewer concurrent options means more available cognitive bandwidth for the decision itself.
LAW 02 · Hick

Decision time scales with options

The time to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices.
Hick's original formula is logarithmic: T = b · log₂(n+1). Each added option increases decision time measurably. Four options take roughly 32% longer to resolve than two — friction the conversion funnel cannot afford.
LAW 03 · Von Restorff

Hierarchy requires restraint

When multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs is remembered.
A genuinely primary action requires every other action to be visually subordinate. Four equally-weighted buttons in a row produces zero primary actions — the brain has no signal to isolate one as the most important. Restraint is the mechanism.
LAW 04 · Nielsen H8

Every element competes

Every extra unit of information competes with relevant units and diminishes their visibility.
Adding a button does not enlarge the interface's signal — it subtracts visibility from the existing elements. Adding "Swap" next to "Deposit" doesn't extend functionality; it dilutes the deposit invitation that the KPI depends on.
LAW 05 · Pareto

Most users use few features

80% of effects come from 20% of causes. 80% of users use 20% of features.
For Peak Money — whose KPI #3 is Deposit Conversion — "Add money" should absorb 80% of the primary action weight. Anything competing for that weight is mathematically counterproductive to the metric the company is graded on.
LAW 06 · Occam

Simpler wins

Between two designs that fulfill the same function, the simpler is better.
The closing rule. If the same job can be done with fewer elements, do it with fewer. Tesler's Law reminds us that complexity isn't eliminated when we hide it from the screen — it's moved off the user's shoulders and onto the designer's.
Six independent psychology principles converging on the same ceiling is not an opinion — it's a finding. Synthesis · UX Forensics Chapter II
02 The ideal numbers

There is no magic number. There is a calibrated range.

The frameworks don't give a universal answer because the right count depends on what category of element is being counted. Combining the six laws above, the calibrated ranges fall out cleanly:

Element category
Ideal count
Cross-law justification
Singular primary CTA
— the hero action
1
Von Restorff requires one element to stand out. This is the strictest constraint for hero CTAs: a "primary" action that has visual peers is, by definition, no longer primary.
Quick actions bar
— top of dashboard
2–3
Hick penalises every addition logarithmically. Pareto suggests 1–2 actions usually absorb 80% of traffic. Three is the reasonable ceiling for a simple dashboard.
Tab bar navigation
— bottom or top tabs
3–5
Miller (4–7 in working memory). Apple HIG and Material Design both recommend 3–5 tabs. More than 5 forces grouping.
Dropdown menu items
— flat list, no submenu
5–7
Miller hard ceiling. Beyond seven, users stop processing the menu as a single decision and resort to scanning — which defeats the recognition heuristic.
Simultaneous decisions per screen
— total cognitive load
≤ 7
Miller absolute. This is the upper bound before the screen overloads working memory and decision quality collapses.
For the specific case of a quick-actions bar in a fintech dashboard, the convergence points at 2–3 actions as the safe range. Four is defensible only if all four belong to the same mental frame (see §04). Peak Money's four don't.

04 Field verification — beyond Mobbin

The big five. One nuance that matters.

Mobbin's sample skews toward smaller and US-centric apps. To stress-test the conclusion, the analysis was widened to five of the largest consumer fintechs by users — Robinhood, Wealthfront, Cash App, Revolut, Chase — sourced from product documentation, design critiques, and the apps' own public materials.

Robinhood

0 global · contextual only

Despite trading frequency being Robinhood's commercial engine, there is no prominent "Trade" button on the home. Buy / Sell / Options surface contextually inside individual stock screens, never as a global quick action bar.

Pattern: contextual actions, not global quick actions.

Wealthfront

0 on dashboard

Wealthfront's home is a balance-and-chart surface. Quick actions sit in a separate tab entirely. The hero of the home screen is the net worth trajectory — not the verbs for moving money.

Pattern: maximum focus on the balance hero.

Cash App

2 primary actions

Radical minimalism: balance hero on top, bottom tab bar (5 tabs) for navigation. When the user does need to act on money, two buttons exist: Add Cash and Cash Out. The cleanest application of Hick's Law in the field.

Pattern: extreme restraint, the textbook example.

Chase Mobile

1 prominent + contextual chips

The home surfaces account cards, each carrying a contextual CTA ("Pay bills", "Send with Zelle"). The recent redesign for ages 18–24 emphasises "quicker access to recent transactions, prominent Zelle access" — i.e. one prominent action, not a row of four.

Pattern: contextual chips per account, not a global bar.

Revolut is the four-action exception. And it teaches the real lesson.

Revolut runs a row of four to five circular icons on its home — typically Add money, Exchange, Transfer, Details, More. By the count alone, this looks like a counter-example to the argument made above. It isn't.

Revolut's four-to-five actions all belong to the same mental frame: money movement. The user processes them as a single decision ("what do I want to do with my money?") with several options of the same kind — not as four cognitively distinct decisions.

Peak Money's four actions belong to four different mental frames. The user's brain has to context-switch between savings, savings, crypto-DeFi, and lottery in the span of a single horizontal scan. Hick's Law is bad enough at four options of one kind; across four frames, the cost compounds.

Revolut · 4 actions, 1 frame
  • Add money — money movement
  • Exchange — money movement
  • Transfer — money movement
  • Details — money movement
Peak Money · 4 actions, 4 frames
  • Deposit — savings frame
  • Withdraw — savings frame
  • Swap — crypto frame (kill-list)
  • Win Prizes — lottery frame
— Verdict —

Peak Money should ship with two primary actions.

The number alone is not the problem — Revolut proves that. The compound problem is that Peak Money has both a count above the canonical ceiling and a spread across four conflicting frames.

Two of those four ("Swap", "Win Prizes") explicitly violate the locked lexicon from the customer-surface brief — "Swap" is on the kill-list, and "Win Prizes" promotes a sweepstakes feature to a peer of the core savings verbs.

The cognitive cost is paid by P1 (Savings Optimizer) on every page view. The conversion cost is paid by KPI #3 (Deposit Conversion). The brand cost is paid in every comparison against Wealthfront and Marcus — the shelf where Peak Money claims it competes.

The convergent evidence — six cognitive laws, ten Mobbin references, five field competitors — all point at the same recommendation. Reduce to two primary actions in the dashboard surface: Add money and Withdraw.

This is the Cash App / SoFi / Marcus pattern. It satisfies Miller, Hick, Von Restorff, Nielsen H8, Pareto, and Occam simultaneously. It returns the dashboard to a single frame (savings account). It puts the strategic action (Add money) in singular Von Restorff position — the only yellow button in the viewport.

The two demoted actions don't disappear: "Win Prizes" returns to its own PLA card on the side, where the sweepstakes register lives natively. "Swap" exits the customer surface entirely, as the brief requires.

— Recommended configuration —

Two primary actions. One frame. One Von Restorff signal.

The defensible configurations for Peak Money's dashboard, in order of how strongly they satisfy the cognitive convergence and the locked lexicon:

Option A — Recommended
Add money   ·   Withdraw
Two actions, one frame. Add money in primary yellow (singular Von Restorff). Withdraw secondary. PLA & history surface as cards below. Matches Cash App, SoFi, Marcus.
